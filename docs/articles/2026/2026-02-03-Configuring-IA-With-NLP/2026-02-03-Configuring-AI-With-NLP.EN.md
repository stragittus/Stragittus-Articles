# Configuring AI with NLP (Neuro‑Linguistic Programming)

## Self‑modeling, decision frameworks, and blind spots

*Version française ci‑dessus.*

---

I’ll start simply, as if we were talking over a meal.

For a long time, I used AI the way many people around me do: with curiosity, enthusiasm, and a fair amount of tinkering. I refined my prompts. I added rules. I specified tone. I asked for more structure, less fluff, more caution.

And it worked. Well… **it worked sometimes**.

But something kept bothering me.

From one conversation to the next, the AI’s posture would shift. One day, very optimization‑driven. The next, much more cautious. On the same topic, its trade‑offs varied. Not in an absurd way—but not in a stable one either.

At some point, I asked myself a very down‑to‑earth question:

> *If a human colleague reasoned like this, would I say they lack skills… or that they lack a framework?*

The answer was obvious.

---

## What NLP helped me put into words

NLP didn’t help me “communicate better with AI.” It helped me **name a problem I felt but couldn’t clearly articulate**.

In NLP, we say that *the map is not the territory*. And above all, that **our decisions always depend on the map we’re using—even when we’re not aware of it**.

With AI, I realized I was making the same mistake many people do: I was changing behaviors (the answers) without ever making explicit the **model of the world** those answers were supposed to fit into.

A prompt is a one‑off instruction.
But it says nothing fundamental about:

* what is a priority,
* what is unacceptable,
* what must remain stable over time.

In other words, I was giving orders… **without giving a compass**.

---

## The intention that triggered everything

The real shift happened when I formulated a very simple—and rather uncomfortable—intention:

> *I want the AI to reason like me when I’m at my best.*

Not faster.
Not smarter.
Not more “intelligent.”

But with **the same decision criteria**.

And at that point, there’s no room for shortcuts. Because it forces a real question:

> *When I make serious decisions, what do I actually base them on?*

Not what I say.
Not what I’d like to believe.
But what I do **when it really matters**.

---

## Self‑modeling (no frills)

I started very concretely. I observed my own decision patterns.

For example:

* If a solution is elegant but increases mental load → I reject it.
* If an optimization helps today but makes tomorrow more rigid → I reject it.
* If I don’t fully understand what I’m proposing → I slow down.

These aren’t lofty philosophical values.
They’re **observable, repeated reflexes**.

Only then did I translate them into NLP terms:

* **Beliefs**: what isn’t made explicit becomes arbitrary.
* **Values**: clarity before sophistication; long term before short‑term gain.
* **Meta‑programs**: error prevention, preference for structure, long‑term orientation.
* **Identity**: the AI as a co‑pilot, never a decision‑maker.

That’s how what I now call a **meta‑role** emerged.

---

## A foundation, not a profession

The meta‑role is not a developer role, a consultant role, or a therapist role.

It’s the **foundation**: the baseline map of the world.

Only then can I say:

> *From this framework, reason like a senior software developer.*

Or, in another context:

> *From this framework, adopt a more reflective, therapeutic posture.*

The specific role refines expression.
The meta‑role ensures coherence.

---

## Logical levels, used for real

NLP logical levels didn’t remain a theoretical diagram.

Whenever a response felt “off,” I asked myself a simple question:

* Is this a **behavior** issue?
* A **capability** issue?
* A **belief** issue?
* Or a **value** issue?

Nine times out of ten, the problem was **above the prompt level**.

---

## Long‑term horizon and contexts

Two other elements naturally imposed themselves.

First, an **explicit horizon**: 2038. Not as a numerical target, but as a reference point. A decision is only valid if it remains sound over time.

Second, **clearly separated contexts** (professional, personal, family, children…). Without exposing their content—just acknowledging that they exist. Because the same question, in a different context, calls for a different answer.

This isn’t a detail. It’s what prevents generic or misplaced responses.

---

## When the framework becomes too comfortable

After a while, I noticed something else.

The answers were coherent. Very coherent.

Maybe **too** coherent.

Always the same cautions.
Always the same priorities.

I had created a bias. An intentional one—but a bias nonetheless.

That’s when **SHADOW** appeared.

SHADOW is the deliberate activation of an opposite framework. Different values. Different priorities. Different angles.

Not to stay there.
But to see **what my main framework was excluding**.

With hindsight, I see SHADOW as something fundamentally different from the meta‑role.

The **METAROLE** operates mainly at the conscious level: structure, values, criteria, decisions.

**SHADOW** touches less conscious layers. Sometimes more emotional. More raw. The ones we tend to set aside because they disrupt the frame.

When I activate SHADOW, the answers change noticeably.

They’re often more direct. Sometimes uncomfortable. Less diplomatic.

But also more authentic—more aligned with a kind of truth that the main framework tends to smooth out.

It’s not always pleasant.
But it’s often right.

---

## A simple, conscious gesture

Today, when I start a conversation, I almost always do the same thing.

I begin by making the framework explicit:

* `/METAROLE` when I want aligned, stable reasoning.
* `/SHADOW` when I want to introduce deliberate friction.

These aren’t technical commands.
They’re **conscious framing acts**.

And yes, I can switch frameworks mid‑conversation.

---

## Treating the framework like a real system

At that point, one thing became obvious to me: if this framework matters, it has to be maintainable.

Today, the meta‑role is:

* a YAML file of a little over **300 lines**,
* versioned in Git,
* used as the single source of truth,
* rendered into Markdown, then shortened into *custom instructions*,
* and attached to concrete AI projects.

Not for aesthetics.
But so I can correct, roll back, and adjust.

---

## What if it weren’t only personal?

Once this work was in place, another idea began to surface.

Not as a certainty. More as a curiosity.

> *What if the same approach could apply to someone else? To a team? To an organization?*

For now, this remains largely **theoretical** for me. I haven’t seriously tested it yet.

But intuitively, it makes sense.

Mission, Vision, and Values are already a form of modeling. AI simply forces us to go one step further: making them **operational**, usable in real trade‑offs.

Not to replace human judgment, but to **make explicit what usually remains implicit**.

It’s clearly a space I want to explore.

---

## What this gives me today

With the meta‑role in place, something has changed quite noticeably.

I reformulate my questions far less. I spend less time correcting the framework, restating implicit rules, or clarifying expectations.

The answers get to the point faster. They’re shorter, more direct, and—above all—more consistent from one conversation to the next.

There’s also an effect that’s harder to measure, but very real: **less mental fatigue**. I’m no longer fighting the tool. I’m dialoguing with it within a framework I recognize.

I also trust the answers more—not because they’re always right, but because I understand **where they’re coming from**.

And that profoundly changes the relationship.

---

## In closing

Configuring AI, for me, is no longer about optimization.

It’s a matter of **coherence**.

Between what I say I want.
And what I actually do when I decide.

AI has become a somewhat demanding mirror. It forces me to make my frameworks explicit, to acknowledge my biases, to accept my blind spots.

And paradoxically, that’s what makes it more reliable.

Not because it’s more intelligent.

But because it reasons, as much as possible, **within the same world as I do**.
